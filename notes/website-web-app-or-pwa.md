Almost every project I take on starts with the same sentence: "I need a website." Sometimes that is exactly right. Often it is not, and figuring that out early saves a business a lot of money and a lot of frustration.

So before I write a single line of code, I ask one question: what do you want people to be able to *do*?

## When a website is enough

A website's job is to inform and convince. Someone hears about your business, looks you up, and within a few seconds decides whether you are worth a call. A good website answers their questions quickly: what you offer, why you are different, how much it roughly costs, and how to reach you.

If your customers mostly read, compare and then call or message you, a fast, well designed website is the right tool. It is also the cheapest to build and the easiest to maintain. A church, a consultancy, a restaurant or a small NGO usually lives here.

## When you actually need a web app

The moment people need to log in and get something done, you have moved into web app territory. Booking an appointment, managing stock, approving leave, tracking orders, taking payments, issuing certificates. These are tasks, not pages.

Web apps need things websites never think about: user accounts, permissions, a database that stays consistent, and a design that makes repeated work fast. A school portal, an employee system or a point of sale system all belong here. They cost more to build, but they replace spreadsheets, paperwork and hours of manual work every week.

## The middle ground: progressive web apps

A progressive web app, or PWA, is a web app that behaves like an app on a phone. Users can add it to their home screen, it opens full screen, it loads quickly and it can keep working when the network is poor.

For many businesses in Ghana and across Africa, this is the sweet spot. You get one product that works on Android, iPhone and desktop, updates instantly without an app store, and does not eat up your customers' storage. I reach for a PWA whenever a client wants "an app" but mainly needs it for forms, dashboards or ordering.

## When a dedicated app is worth it

Some jobs really do need a native mobile or desktop app. If you depend on the camera, Bluetooth printers, barcode scanners, long periods offline, or tight integration with the device, a dedicated app built with something like Flutter or Electron earns its extra cost. A POS that must keep selling when the internet drops is a good example.

## A simple way to decide

Here is the rough guide I use with clients:

- **People mostly read and then contact you:** a website.
- **People log in and complete tasks:** a web app.
- **Those tasks happen mostly on phones:** a PWA.
- **You need the device's hardware or long offline use:** a dedicated mobile or desktop app.

You do not have to pick the "biggest" option to be taken seriously. The best choice is the simplest one that fully solves the problem, with room to grow later.
